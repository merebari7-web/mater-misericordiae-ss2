Definition of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms or microbes are microscopic organisms, mostly unicellular or 
acellular that are too small to be seen with the naked human eye. They require 
a light or electron microscope for observation. 
They are ubiquitous (found everywhere), in soil, aquatic environments (fresh 
water and marine), air, extreme environments (hot springs, deep ocean vents) 
and as endosymbionts or parasites inside other organisms. The branch of 
biology that entails the study of microorganisms is known as MICROBIOLOGY 
Classification of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms are grouped based on cellular structure (prokaryotic, 
eukaryotic, or non-cellular), nutritional modes, and genetic material. 
1. Bacteria: They are microorganism that can be seen with a light 
microscope. 
a. Cell Structure: Unicellular prokaryotes (lacking a membrane bound 
nucleus and organelle structures). 
b. Cell Wall Composition: Peptidoglycan (murein). 
c. Morphological Shapes: Cocci (spherical)e.g Staphylococcus aureus, Bacilli 
(rod shaped) Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Spirilla(spiral/twisted) e.g 
Treponema pallidum, Vibrio (comma shaped) e.g Vibrio cholerae. 
2. Viruses: They exist on the biological borderline between the living and 
non-living world. They are acellular entities (they lack cellular structures 
like a cytoplasm, cell membrane or ribosomes. 
They cannot perform metabolic processes or reproduce on their own, they 
are classified as obligate intracellular parasite: they must invade a living 
host cell to replicate. Outside a host, they behave like non-living particles. 
Examples include Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), Influenza Virus, 
Rabies Virus, Bacteriophages. 
3. Microscopic Fungi 
i) Cell Structure: Eukaryotic (containing a true nucleus and bound 
organelles), can be unicellular(yeasts) or multicellular filament

iv) Examples include: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Baker’s yeast), Rhizopus

Meaning and Principles of Culturing Microorganisms 
Microbial Culturing is the controlled laboratory technique of growing and 
multiplying microorganisms in designated nutrient media under favorable 
environmental conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen levels). 
Concepts in Culturing 
Culture Medium: A nutrient liquid broth or solid gel formulated with essential 
carbon, nitrogen, mineral salts, and vitamins required for microbial growth. 
Agar: A complex polysaccharide extracted from red algae, used as a solidifying 
agent in culture media due to its inertness to microbial digestion. 
Aseptic Technique: Sterilization protocols (e.g., autoclaving at 121°C/15 psi, 
flaming inoculation loops) used to prevent unwanted microbial contamination. 
Pure Culture: A culture medium containing only a single, isolated species or 
strain of microorganism. 
Importance & Applications of Culturing 
1. Medical Diagnostics: Isolating and identifying pathogenic bacteria or fungi 
from patient samples (blood, urine, swab) to determine antibiotic sensitivity. 
2. Pharmaceutical Production: Mass cultivation of Penicillium species for 
commercial antibiotic extraction. 
3. Industrial Fermentation: Industrial culturing of Saccharomyces for 
brewing and baking, or Lactobacillus for dairy products (yogurt, cheese). 
Pathogens, Carriers, and Transmitting Organisms 
Microorganisms that cause diseases in plants and animals are called 
pathogens. Pathogens rely on specific reservoirs and transmission vectors to 
spread between hosts. 
Disease Carriers 
A carrier is an infected individual (human or animal) who harbors a specific 
pathogen without showing overt clinical signs or symptoms, yet remains 
capable of transmitting the pathogen to susceptible individuals. 
Asymptomatic Carrier: Harbors the pathogen continuously without 
experiencing active illness (e.g., individuals carrying Salmonella typhi causing 
Typhoid fever). 
Convalescent Carrier: Continues to shed and transmit the pathogen while 
recovering from an infection. 
Vectors & Transmitting Organisms 
A vector is an intermediate host organism (usually an arthropod) that 
transfers a pathogenic microorganism from an infected host or reservoir to a 
susceptible host.

Beneficial and Harmful Effects of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms (microbes) are microscopic organisms such as bacteria, 
viruses, fungi, protozoa, and microscopic algae. While many are pathogenic 
(disease-causing), numerous microbes play essential roles in medicine, 
agriculture, food processing, and environmental sustainability.

Medicine & Health: 
• Antibiotic Production: Fungi such as Penicillium chrysogenum yield 
penicillin; Streptomyces species produce streptomycin. 
• Vaccines & Sera: Attenuated (weakened) or killed microbes stimulate 
active immunity against severe pathogens. 
• Gut Microbiota: Normal intestinal bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli) 
synthesize Vitamin K and Vitamin B complex while suppressing 
pathogenic invaders. 
Agriculture & Soil Fertility: 
• Nitrogen Fixation: Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules and free
living Azotobacter convert atmospheric nitrogen into soil nitrates. 
• Decomposition & Nutrient Cycling: Saprophytic bacteria and fungi 
break down dead organic matter, recycling essential elements (carbon, 
nitrogen, phosphorus). 
Industry & Biotechnology: 
Food & Beverage Processing: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast) ferments 
sugars into ethanol and CO₂ (baking and brewing). Lactobacillus species 
ferment lactose to lactic acid for yogurt and cheese production. 
Sewage Treatment: Aerobic bacteria digest dissolved organic wastes in 
municipal sewage purification plants. 
Harmful Effects of Microorganisms 
1. Human & Animal Diseases: Pathogens cause infections such as cholera 
(Vibrio cholerae), tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis), malaria 
(Plasmodium spp.), and influenza. 
2. Plant Diseases: Microbes cause significant crop damage, e.g., cassava 
mosaic virus, maize rust, and potato late blight (Phytophthora infestans). 
3. Food Spoilage: Saprophytic bacteria and molds decompose raw and cooked 
food. Clostridium botulinum releases lethal neurotoxins causing botulism. 
4.Material Deterioration: Cellulolytic bacteria and fungi degrade timber, 
paper, textiles, and leather goods. 
Control of Harmful Microorganisms & Public Health 
Controlling microbial growth eliminates pathogens and halts disease 
transmission cycles within communities. Methods are categorized into 
physical, chemical, and public health measures. 
Disease transmission cycle and critical public health intervention points. 
Physical Control Methods 
High Temperature: Boiling water (100°C for 10–15 mins) kills vegetative 
bacterial cells. Autoclaving uses moist steam under pressure (121°C for 15 
mins) to destroy heat-resistant bacterial endospores. 
Pasteurization: Heating milk/liquids to 72°C for 15 seconds followed by rapid 
cooling eliminates pathogens without destroying flavor or nutritional value. 
Low Temperature & Dehydration: Refrigeration (4°C) and freezing (-18°C) 
exert a bacteriostatic effect (halting growth). Desiccation removes moisture 
necessary for enzymatic activity. 
Radiation: Ultraviolet (UV) radiation and gamma rays sterilize surgical 
instruments, pharmaceuticals, and packaged food. 
Chemical Control Methods 
Disinfectants: Strong chemical agents applied to non-living inanimate 
surfaces to destroy microbes (e.g., bleach, Lysol, phenol). 
Antiseptics: Mild chemical solutions safe for application on living tissue and 
wounds to prevent infection (e.g., 70% ethanol, iodine, hydrogen peroxide). 
Preservatives: Table salt, sugar, and vinegar inhibit microbial activity via 
osmotic dehydration or by lowering pH. 
Public Health Measures & Vector Control 
Safe Water Provision: Purification of municipal water through sedimentation, 
filtration, and chlorination. 
Proper Waste Disposal: Underground sewage systems, sanitary pit latrines, 
and managed landfills prevent insect vector breeding. 
Vector Eradication: Use of Long-Lasting Insecticide-treated Nets (LLINs) and 
indoor spraying against mosquitoes; food protection against houseflies (Musca 
domestica). 
Immunization Programs: Routine mass vaccination builds herd immunity 
against polio, measles, diphtheria, and yellow fever. 
Quarantine & Isolation: Enforced restriction of infected individuals or animal 
products during epidemic disease outbreaks. 
Maintenance of Good Health 
Good health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. 
Maintaining health relies on three primary pillars: 
Personal Hygiene: Frequent handwashing with soap under clean running 
water (especially before meals and after toilet use). Daily bathing, oral hygiene 
twice daily, and keeping fingernails trimmed short to eliminate dirt and 
parasite eggs. 
Environmental Sanitation: Keeping living spaces, kitchens, and toilets clean 
and well-ventilated. Disposing of domestic refuse in covered bins and draining 
stagnant water pools to prevent mosquito breeding. 
Healthy Lifestyle Factors: Consuming a balanced diet rich in essential 
micronutrients (Vitamin C, Zinc) to fortify immunity. Engaging in regular 
physical exercise and ensuring 7–8 hours of restful sleep daily for tissue 
recovery. 
Roles of Health Organizations 
1.World Health Organization (WHO): This is a specialized division of the United 
Nations Organization (UNO), now known as the United Nations, established in 
1948 with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland. Its major aim is to improve the 
health of the people in all the countries of the world. 
2.United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF): The UNICEF, another special 
agency of the United Nations Organistion, now known as the United Nations, 
was set up to improve the health and welfare of the children all over the world. 
3.Nigerian Medical Association (NMA) 
The Nigerian Medical Association (NMA) is a national body concerned with the 
maintenance of good health within Nigeria.

Characteristics/features of Marine Habitat 
A marine habitat is an aquatic environment composed of saltwater, 
encompassing all oceans and seas. It covers approximately 70% of the earth's 
surface and serves as the largest global aquatic biome, supporting diverse 
ecosystems. 
Characteristics of Marine Habitat 
The marine or salt water habitats possess the following key physical and 
chemical characteristics: 
1. Salinity: Salinity is defined as the degree of saltiness or concentration of 
salt solution in oceans. Marine habitats have high salinity, with an average 
salinity put at 35.2 per 1000 (35.2 parts of salts by weight per 1000 parts of 
water). 
2. Density: The density of marine water is high (~1.028) compared to fresh 
water (1.00). Hence, many organisms can easily float on ocean water. 
3. Pressure: Water pressure increases with depth at the rate of one 
atmosphere for every 10 meters, varying from 1 atm at the surface to about 
1000 atm at extreme depths. 
4. Size: Marine habitats represent the largest of all habitats. Oceans alone 
occupy over 70% (approx. 360 million km²) of the Earth's total surface area 
(510 million km²). Examples include the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. 
5. Currents: Currents are produced by wind action at the surface and by 
internal variations in salinity and temperature deeper in the ocean. 
6. Tides: Tides are the periodic rise and fall of the ocean surface (occurring 
roughly twice daily), caused by the gravitational attraction of the moon and 
sun. 
7. Oxygen Concentration: Highest at the surface level where atmospheric 
exchange occurs; oxygen concentration decreases with depth, becoming 
practically non-existent in extreme depths. 
8. Hydrogen Ion Concentration (pH): Seawater is alkaline in nature with a 
typical pH ranging between 8.0 and 9.0 near the surface. 
9. Waves: Movements of surface waters caused by winds that facilitate 
continuous mixing of seawater near the surface. 
10. Light Penetration: Light penetrates ocean water up to a maximum depth 
of about 200 meters (Euphotic zone), directly limiting plant life to these upper 
illuminated layers.

Distribution of organisms in habitats  
Organisms in marine environments are distributed into three main ecological 
groups: 
1. Plankton (Floating organisms): Drifting microscopic organisms found 
mainly in the lighted upper zone. 
Phytoplankton (Producers): Microscopic plant-like algae (e.g., Diatoms, 
Dinoflagellates) that carry out photosynthesis. 
Zooplankton (Consumers): Microscopic animals (e.g., Protozoa, 
Copepods, jellyfish larvae). 
2. Nekton (Active swimmers): Animals capable of swimming strongly 
against water currents (e.g., Tilapia, Tuna, Sharks, Whales, Sea Turtles, 
Squids). 
3. Benthos (Bottom dwellers): Organisms attached to or crawling on the 
sea floor (e.g., Sea Anemones, Corals, Sea Urchins, Crabs, Starfish, Sea 
Cucumbers). 
Adaptive features of marine organisms  
Plant Adaptations 
Salt Water Swamp Plants: Possess succulent stems/roots to dilute salt 
concentration. 
Have pneumatophores (breathing roots) for atmospheric gas exchange. 
Stilt roots provide structural anchorage in mud. 
Viviparous seedlings ensure survival and continuity. 
Excrete excess salt to maintain internal body salt balance. 
Seaweeds: Holdfast structures for firm attachment to rocks. 
Mucilaginous outer layer prevents desiccation. 
Divided leaves and air bladders for buoyancy in water. 
Animal Adaptations 
Barnacles: Possess protective mantles or attachment pads to firmly anchor 
onto rocks, preventing desiccation during low tide. 
Cartilaginous Fishes (e.g., Sharks, Dogfish): Maintain high internal salt 
balance and body osmotic pressure to adapt to high ocean salinity. 
Bony Fishes (e.g., Tilapia, Herring): Drink salt water to cope with high 
salinity and utilize salt-secreting glands in their gills or eyes to excrete excess 
salt. 
Shrimps: Equipped with powerful chelipeds (claws) for capturing and 
holding prey. 
Crabs: Burrow rapidly into coastal mud to protect against wave action, 
tides, and predators. 
Periwinkles: Possess modified lungs for breathing air during exposure and 
a strong muscular foot for firm attachment. 
Starfish: Utilize tube feet to hold securely onto rocky shores and hard 
outer shells to prevent desiccation.

Types of Estuarine Habitat 
An estuarine habitat (or estuary) is a semi-enclosed coastal body of water 
where fresh water from rivers and streams meets and mixes with salt water 
from the ocean. This mixing produces brackish water, which has a salinity 
intermediate between fresh water (<0.5‰) and full seawater (~35‰). 
Estuaries are highly dynamic transition zones (ecotones) affected by tidal 
fluctuations, high nutrient loads, and fluctuating environmental parameters. 
Types of Estuaries 
1. Delta: A delta is where a river divides into many channels before entry 
into the ocean or sea. Brackish water or estuary (delta) is formed at the 
mouth of a river as it enters the sea. 
2. Lagoon: This is a body of ocean water that enters into the land through a 
canal and therefore has the opportunity of mixing with fresh water from 
rivers and streams. 
3. Bay: This is a little or small portion of the sea water which enters into 
the land and mixes up with fresh water from rivers and streams. A bay is 
very small and not long enough to rejoin the sea at another end. 
Characteristics of Estuarine Habitats 
The following are the principal characteristics of estuarine habitats: 
(1) Fluctuation in salinity: Salinity fluctuates in this habitat. Salinity is 
lower at the mouth of a river and gets higher towards the sea. Salinity is 
also affected by season: while the rainy season reduces salinity due to the 
addition of fresh water, the dry season increases it. 
(2) Turbidity: Turbidity of estuarine habitats increases especially during 
the rainy season when lots of debris are brought down by rivers to the 
habitat. This high turbidity also reduces the rate of photosynthesis and 
respiration by organisms. 
(3) Shallowness of water: Unlike sea water which is deep, the water in an 
estuarine habitat is generally very shallow. 
(4) Low species diversity: The estuarine habitat has low diversity of 
species compared to marine habitats. Common plant species include 
phytoplankton, algae, and marsh vegetation, while animal species include 
crabs, oysters, lobsters, etc. 
(5) Water is affected by tides: Sea water usually flows rapidly into 
estuaries at high tides and rushes back into the ocean at low tides. 
(6) High level of nutrients: The estuarine habitats contain abundant 
nutrients, especially organic detritus, which form the bulk of producers in 
the habitat. 
(7) Low oxygen content: Oxygen content of estuarine habitats is generally 
very low and, as a result, much of the microbiological activity is anaerobic. 
Distribution of Plants and Animals in Estuarine Habitats 
Plant Species and Their Adaptive Features 
(1) Planktons (diatoms): They possess air spaces in their tissues, rhizoids 
or false feet for attachment to rocky shores, and air bladders for buoyancy. 
(2) Algae: They possess chlorophyll for photosynthetic activities and a small 
size or large surface-area-to-volume ratio for floating. 
(3) Red mangrove (Rhizophora racemosa): It has stilt roots which grow 
down from the stem into the soft mud and develop numerous rootlets with 
air spaces for conducting air to root tissues. The roots also provide support 
and prevent plants from being washed away by tides. Furthermore, the 
seeds of red mangrove germinate while still on the parent plant (vivipary), 
preventing seedlings from being carried away by water currents. 
(4) White mangrove (Avicennia nitida): It has pneumatophores (breathing 
roots) for gaseous exchange. 
Animal Species and Their Adaptive Features 
(1) Mosquito larvae and pupae: These organisms possess breathing 
trumpets for gaseous exchange. 
(2) Crabs: They can burrow fast into the mud for protection against 
predators, strong waves, or tides. 
(3) Water snails and shrimps: These animals can burrow into the mud 
when the tide is going out, thus escaping the periodic dilution of their 
external medium. 
(4) Worms: They have strong, protective, impermeable coverings against 
high salinity. 
(5) Mudskippers: These animals have modified fins adapted for crawling 
when on land and for swimming when in water. 
(6) Fishes: Fishes like Tilapia have fins for movement and a swim bladder 
for buoyancy control. 
Food Chain in Estuarine Habitats 
A typical food chain in an estuarine habitat may contain up to three, four, or 
five trophic levels. Phytoplankton (such as diatoms) and organic detritus form 
the primary producers supporting the food chain. 
Examples of Food Chains in Estuarine Habitats: 
(i)   Detritus → Worms → Snails → Birds 
(ii)  Diatoms → Shrimps → Fishes 
(iii) Phytoplankton → Barnacles → Fish → Birds

Types and characteristics of fresh water habitat 
Fresh water habitat is a body of water formed mainly from inland waters and 
contain a very low level of salinity. Examples of fresh water habitat are rivers, 
ponds, streams, springs and lakes. Freshwater habitats are classified primarily 
based on the mobility or movement direction of the water body into two distinct 
categories: Lotic (Running) Waters and Lentic (Stagnant) Waters. 
Lotic (Running) Waters: Waters that flow continuously in a specific direction. 
They possess strong water currents, high oxygen concentration due to constant 
aeration, and are generally non-stratified. Examples are Rivers, streams, 
springs, brooks, waterfalls. 
Lentic (Stagnant) Waters: Standing or calm waters that do not flow in a 
specific direction. They accumulate organic sediments, undergo thermal/light 
stratification, and exhibit distinct zonation. Examples are Lakes, ponds, 
swamps, dams, reservoirs, bogs. 
Characteristics of Fresh Water Habitats 
Low Salinity: Freshwater habitats contain very low levels of dissolved salts 
(typically around 0.5% or less), compared to oceans which average about 3.5% 
(35 parts per thousand). 
Small Relative Size: Freshwater habitats occupy a very small proportion of 
Earth's total water surface compared to marine habitats, which cover over 75% 
of the Earth's surface. 
Temperature Variations: Temperature fluctuates with seasonal changes, 
weather, and depth. In lentic bodies, surface water is slightly warmer than 
bottom mud during warm periods. 
High Dissolved Oxygen Content: Oxygen is readily available in most 
freshwater zones, particularly in fast-flowing lotic waters and the surface layer 
of lentic bodies. 
Shallowness: Most freshwater habitats are relatively shallow compared to 
marine oceans, allowing light to easily penetrate to the substrate in many 
areas. 
Seasonal Volume Variation: Streams and rivers experience high volume, swift 
currents, and increased turbidity during rainy seasons, but dry up or shrink 
significantly in dry seasons. 
Current Action: Unidirectional currents in lotic environments distribute 
dissolved gases, salts, nutrients, and microorganisms while acting as a 
physical challenge to non-adapted organisms. 
Ecological Zones of Fresh Water Habitats 
1. Lentic Habitat Zones (e.g., Lakes and Ponds) 
Lentic habitats display two primary vertical ecological zones based on light 
penetration, rooted vegetation, and depth: 
Littoral Zone: The shallow edge/margin of the water body where sunlight 
easily penetrates to the bottom sediment. It features rooted vegetation, warm 
temperatures, maximum dissolved oxygen, and the highest primary biological 
productivity. 
Plants: Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas, water fern, duckweed, water lettuce 
(Pistia), diatoms, sedges. 
Animals: Water fleas, water snails, flatworms, frogs, toads, water skaters 
(Gerri’s), ducks, snakes, crocodiles, tadpoles, Hydra, hippopotamus. 
Benthic Zone: The deep bottom region composed of organic mud and 
substrate (humus). Although rooted vegetation like littoral plants is absent at 
the base, floating plants with deep-anchored roots exist here. Light is limited, 
and organic decomposition is high. 
Plants: Water lily (Nymphaea), water arum, ferns, Crinum lily, Commelina, 
submerged water grasses. 
Animals: Protozoa, rotifers, Hydra, tilapia fish, mudfish, catfish, leeches, 
caddisfly larvae, mosquito larvae/pupae, water spider, crayfish, water scorpion, 
water boatman, water bugs. 
2. Lotic Habitat Zones (e.g., Rivers and Streams) 
In lotic (running) water ecosystems, zones are differentiated by water flow 
velocity and pool depth: 
Pool Zone: Deeper regions of the river where water flow is relatively slow, calm, 
and peaceful. Fine silt and organic matter settle here. 
Rapid Zone: Shallow areas where water flows swiftly over rocky substrates. 
Aeration is maximum, but thermal stratification does not occur due to 
continuous mixing. 
Eutrophication in Freshwater Ecosystems 
Eutrophication is defined as the excessive growth of plant life or algal blooms 
in a body of water, caused by an enrichment of organic nutrients and minerals, 
ultimately leading to severe oxygen depletion and the collapse of aquatic life. 
Major Causes of Eutrophication: 
Overuse of agricultural fertilizers containing nitrates and phosphates, which 
wash into rivers/lakes during heavy rainfall. 
Discharge of untreated domestic sewage and organic waste runoff from fish 
farming operations.

(due to hypotonic water), strong water currents in lotic habitats, and light 
limitations in deep lentic habitats. Below are their key structural, physiological,

Food Chains and Energy Flow in Fresh Waters 
Freshwater Food Chains 
Diatoms (Producer) → Fish fry (Primary Consumer) → Tilapia (Secondary 
Consumer) 
Detritus (Organic waste) → Worm (Primary Consumer) → Shrimps (Secondary 
Consumer) → Water Bird (Tertiary Consumer) 
Spirogyra (Producer) → Tadpoles (Primary Consumer) → Carps (Secondary 
Consumer) → Kingfisher (Tertiary Consumer) 
Energy Flow Pathway 
Radiant Solar Energy is absorbed by green plants and phytoplankton 
(producers) for photosynthesis to synthesize organic carbohydrates. 
Herbivores (zooplankton, tadpoles) consume producers directly (primary 
consumer level). 
Carnivores (small/large fish, water birds) obtain energy indirectly by feeding 
on herbivores. 
Decomposers (saprophytic fungi and bacteria) break down organic wastes and 
dead bodies, releasing inorganic nutrients back into the mud. 
Energy Loss: At each trophic transfer level, a significant proportion of energy is 
lost as heat through respiration and metabolic activities. 
Factors Affecting Fresh Water Habitats 
Freshwater ecosystems are regulated by both biotic and abiotic ecological 
factors: 
Biotic Factors: Producers (green plants, diatoms, algae), Consumers (fish, 
tadpoles, insects, aquatic birds), Parasites, and Decomposers (bacteria, 
saprophytic fungi). 
Abiotic Factors: Water current velocity, temperature, dissolved oxygen 
content, salinity level, water turbidity/clarity, pH, and sunlight penetration 
depth.

Features of Marsh 
Terrestrial habitats refer to life on earth. The nature of soil, rainfall and 
temperature are the major factors affecting the nature of terrestrial habitats. 
Terrestrial habitat is divided into four groups namely: Marsh, Rainforest, 
Savanna or Grassland and Arid.  
Marshy Habitats 
Marshy habitat is defined as a lowland habitat which is usually flooded or 
water logged all the time. Naturally, grasses and shrubs grow in marsh but 
when trees grow there, it is called a swamp. Marsh is often regarded as a 
transition between the aquatic and terrestrial habitats. 
Features of Marsh Habitats: 
1. Waterlogged & Anaerobic Soil: The ground remains saturated with 
water for extended periods, creating an oxygen-depleted (anaerobic) 
underground environment that slows organic decomposition. 
2. Herbaceous Plant Dominance: Trees are largely absent; non-woody 
plants with soft, flexible stems dominate the landscape. 
3. Shallow Water Level: Water depth typically ranges from a few 
centimeters to about a meter, allowing sunlight to reach the substrate 
and support dense vegetation. 
4. High Organic Accumulation: Partially decayed plant material 
accumulates to form nutrient-rich mud, silt, and peat deposits. 
5. Dynamic Salinity Levels: Depending on geographical location, salinity 
can range from completely fresh to brackish or hypersaline coastal water 
Formation of Marshes (Ecological Succession) 
Stage 1: Sediment Accumulation: Slow moving rivers, streams, or coastal tides 
deposit fine silt, clay, and organic debris in low lying shallow water basins. 
Stage 2: Shallowing of water: Continuous deposition reduces water depth, 
enabling sunlight to penetrate the basin floor and creating ideal conditions for 
plant attachment. 
Stage 3: Pioneer Colonization: water tolerant herbaceous species (e.g. reeds, 
sedges, cattails) take root in the soft mud, Marshes develop gradually through 
a process of ecological succession in areas with poor drainage and continuous 
sediment input: stabilizing the sediment with their root networks. 
Stage 4: Organic Maturation: As plant material dies and decays, organic peat 
accumulates, raising the ground level and establishing a mature marsh 
ecosystem. 
Types of Marshes 
There are two types of marshes. These are freshwater marshes and salt water 
marshes. 
Freshwater marshes: Fresh water marshes occur on land, just beyond the 
limits of the salt water marshes and beyond the areas influenced by tide. In 
this area, fresh water from rivers overflows the river banks to flood the 
adjoining lowlands resulting in the formation of fresh water marshes. 
Saltwater marshes: Saltwater marshes occur along the coastal areas and they 
are influenced by tides because the water along the coast is salty, it mixes up 
with fresh water from rivers to form brackish water. The action of tide in the 
ocean causes the flooding of adjoining lowlands with brackish water resulting 
in the formation of salt water marshes.

Definition of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms or microbes are microscopic organisms, mostly unicellular or 
acellular that are too small to be seen with the naked human eye. They require 
a light or electron microscope for observation. 
They are ubiquitous (found everywhere), in soil, aquatic environments (fresh 
water and marine), air, extreme environments (hot springs, deep ocean vents) 
and as endosymbionts or parasites inside other organisms. The branch of 
biology that entails the study of microorganisms is known as MICROBIOLOGY 
Classification of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms are grouped based on cellular structure (prokaryotic, 
eukaryotic, or non-cellular), nutritional modes, and genetic material. 
1. Bacteria: They are microorganism that can be seen with a light 
microscope. 
a. Cell Structure: Unicellular prokaryotes (lacking a membrane bound 
nucleus and organelle structures). 
b. Cell Wall Composition: Peptidoglycan (murein). 
c. Morphological Shapes: Cocci (spherical)e.g Staphylococcus aureus, Bacilli 
(rod shaped) Bacillus subtilis, Escherichia coli, Spirilla(spiral/twisted) e.g 
Treponema pallidum, Vibrio (comma shaped) e.g Vibrio cholerae. 
2. Viruses: They exist on the biological borderline between the living and 
non-living world. They are acellular entities (they lack cellular structures 
like a cytoplasm, cell membrane or ribosomes. 
They cannot perform metabolic processes or reproduce on their own, they 
are classified as obligate intracellular parasite: they must invade a living 
host cell to replicate. Outside a host, they behave like non-living particles. 
Examples include Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), Influenza Virus, 
Rabies Virus, Bacteriophages.

Meaning and Principles of Culturing Microorganisms 
Microbial Culturing is the controlled laboratory technique of growing and 
multiplying microorganisms in designated nutrient media under favorable 
environmental conditions (temperature, pH, oxygen levels). 
Concepts in Culturing 
Culture Medium: A nutrient liquid broth or solid gel formulated with essential 
carbon, nitrogen, mineral salts, and vitamins required for microbial growth. 
Agar: A complex polysaccharide extracted from red algae, used as a solidifying 
agent in culture media due to its inertness to microbial digestion. 
Aseptic Technique: Sterilization protocols (e.g., autoclaving at 121°C/15 psi, 
flaming inoculation loops) used to prevent unwanted microbial contamination. 
Pure Culture: A culture medium containing only a single, isolated species or 
strain of microorganism. 
Importance & Applications of Culturing 
1. Medical Diagnostics: Isolating and identifying pathogenic bacteria or fungi 
from patient samples (blood, urine, swab) to determine antibiotic sensitivity. 
2. Pharmaceutical Production: Mass cultivation of Penicillium species for 
commercial antibiotic extraction. 
3. Industrial Fermentation: Industrial culturing of Saccharomyces for 
brewing and baking, or Lactobacillus for dairy products (yogurt, cheese). 
Pathogens, Carriers, and Transmitting Organisms 
Microorganisms that cause diseases in plants and animals are called 
pathogens. Pathogens rely on specific reservoirs and transmission vectors to 
spread between hosts. 
Disease Carriers 
A carrier is an infected individual (human or animal) who harbors a specific 
pathogen without showing overt clinical signs or symptoms, yet remains 
capable of transmitting the pathogen to susceptible individuals. 
Asymptomatic Carrier: Harbors the pathogen continuously without 
experiencing active illness (e.g., individuals carrying Salmonella typhi causing 
Typhoid fever). 
Convalescent Carrier: Continues to shed and transmit the pathogen while 
recovering from an infection. 
Vectors & Transmitting Organisms 
A vector is an intermediate host organism (usually an arthropod) that 
transfers a pathogenic microorganism from an infected host or reservoir to a 
susceptible host.

Beneficial and Harmful Effects of Microorganisms 
Microorganisms (microbes) are microscopic organisms such as bacteria, 
viruses, fungi, protozoa, and microscopic algae. While many are pathogenic 
(disease-causing), numerous microbes play essential roles in medicine, 
agriculture, food processing, and environmental sustainability.

Medicine & Health: 
• Antibiotic Production: Fungi such as Penicillium chrysogenum yield 
penicillin; Streptomyces species produce streptomycin. 
• Vaccines & Sera: Attenuated (weakened) or killed microbes stimulate 
active immunity against severe pathogens. 
• Gut Microbiota: Normal intestinal bacteria (e.g., Escherichia coli) 
synthesize Vitamin K and Vitamin B complex while suppressing 
pathogenic invaders. 
Agriculture & Soil Fertility: 
• Nitrogen Fixation: Rhizobium bacteria in legume root nodules and free
living Azotobacter convert atmospheric nitrogen into soil nitrates. 
• Decomposition & Nutrient Cycling: Saprophytic bacteria and fungi 
break down dead organic matter, recycling essential elements (carbon, 
nitrogen, phosphorus). 
Industry & Biotechnology: 
Food & Beverage Processing: Saccharomyces cerevisiae (yeast) ferments 
sugars into ethanol and CO₂ (baking and brewing). Lactobacillus species 
ferment lactose to lactic acid for yogurt and cheese production. 
Sewage Treatment: Aerobic bacteria digest dissolved organic wastes in 
municipal sewage purification plants. 
Harmful Effects of Microorganisms 
1. Human & Animal Diseases: Pathogens cause infections such as cholera 
(Vibrio cholerae), tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis), malaria 
(Plasmodium spp.), and influenza. 
2. Plant Diseases: Microbes cause significant crop damage, e.g., cassava 
mosaic virus, maize rust, and potato late blight (Phytophthora infestans). 
3. Food Spoilage: Saprophytic bacteria and molds decompose raw and cooked 
food. Clostridium botulinum releases lethal neurotoxins causing botulism. 
4.Material Deterioration: Cellulolytic bacteria and fungi degrade timber, 
paper, textiles, and leather goods. 
Control of Harmful Microorganisms & Public Health 
Controlling microbial growth eliminates pathogens and halts disease 
transmission cycles within communities. Methods are categorized into 
physical, chemical, and public health measures. 
Disease transmission cycle and critical public health intervention points. 
Physical Control Methods 
High Temperature: Boiling water (100°C for 10–15 mins) kills vegetative 
bacterial cells. Autoclaving uses moist steam under pressure (121°C for 15 
mins) to destroy heat-resistant bacterial endospores. 
Pasteurization: Heating milk/liquids to 72°C for 15 seconds followed by rapid 
cooling eliminates pathogens without destroying flavor or nutritional value. 
Low Temperature & Dehydration: Refrigeration (4°C) and freezing (-18°C) 
exert a bacteriostatic effect (halting growth). Desiccation removes moisture 
necessary for enzymatic activity. 
Radiation: Ultraviolet (UV) radiation and gamma rays sterilize surgical 
instruments, pharmaceuticals, and packaged food. 
Chemical Control Methods 
Disinfectants: Strong chemical agents applied to non-living inanimate 
surfaces to destroy microbes (e.g., bleach, Lysol, phenol). 
Antiseptics: Mild chemical solutions safe for application on living tissue and 
wounds to prevent infection (e.g., 70% ethanol, iodine, hydrogen peroxide). 
Preservatives: Table salt, sugar, and vinegar inhibit microbial activity via 
osmotic dehydration or by lowering pH. 
Public Health Measures & Vector Control 
Safe Water Provision: Purification of municipal water through sedimentation, 
filtration, and chlorination. 
Proper Waste Disposal: Underground sewage systems, sanitary pit latrines, 
and managed landfills prevent insect vector breeding. 
Vector Eradication: Use of Long-Lasting Insecticide-treated Nets (LLINs) and 
indoor spraying against mosquitoes; food protection against houseflies (Musca 
domestica). 
Immunization Programs: Routine mass vaccination builds herd immunity 
against polio, measles, diphtheria, and yellow fever. 
Quarantine & Isolation: Enforced restriction of infected individuals or animal 
products during epidemic disease outbreaks. 
Maintenance of Good Health 
Good health is a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. 
Maintaining health relies on three primary pillars: 
Personal Hygiene: Frequent handwashing with soap under clean running 
water (especially before meals and after toilet use). Daily bathing, oral hygiene 
twice daily, and keeping fingernails trimmed short to eliminate dirt and 
parasite eggs. 
Environmental Sanitation: Keeping living spaces, kitchens, and toilets clean 
and well-ventilated. Disposing of domestic refuse in covered bins and draining 
stagnant water pools to prevent mosquito breeding. 
Healthy Lifestyle Factors: Consuming a balanced diet rich in essential 
micronutrients (Vitamin C, Zinc) to fortify immunity. Engaging in regular 
physical exercise and ensuring 7–8 hours of restful sleep daily for tissue 
recovery. 
Roles of Health Organizations 
1.World Health Organization (WHO): This is a specialized division of the United 
Nations Organization (UNO), now known as the United Nations, established in 
1948 with headquarters in Geneva, Switzerland. Its major aim is to improve the 
health of the people in all the countries of the world. 
2.United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF): The UNICEF, another special 
agency of the United Nations Organistion, now known as the United Nations, 
was set up to improve the health and welfare of the children all over the world. 
3.Nigerian Medical Association (NMA) 
The Nigerian Medical Association (NMA) is a national body concerned with the 
maintenance of good health within Nigeria.

Characteristics/features of Marine Habitat 
A marine habitat is an aquatic environment composed of saltwater, 
encompassing all oceans and seas. It covers approximately 70% of the earth's 
surface and serves as the largest global aquatic biome, supporting diverse 
ecosystems. 
Characteristics of Marine Habitat 
The marine or salt water habitats possess the following key physical and 
chemical characteristics: 
1. Salinity: Salinity is defined as the degree of saltiness or concentration of 
salt solution in oceans. Marine habitats have high salinity, with an average 
salinity put at 35.2 per 1000 (35.2 parts of salts by weight per 1000 parts of 
water). 
2. Density: The density of marine water is high (~1.028) compared to fresh 
water (1.00). Hence, many organisms can easily float on ocean water. 
3. Pressure: Water pressure increases with depth at the rate of one 
atmosphere for every 10 meters, varying from 1 atm at the surface to about 
1000 atm at extreme depths. 
4. Size: Marine habitats represent the largest of all habitats. Oceans alone 
occupy over 70% (approx. 360 million km²) of the Earth's total surface area 
(510 million km²). Examples include the Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. 
5. Currents: Currents are produced by wind action at the surface and by 
internal variations in salinity and temperature deeper in the ocean. 
6. Tides: Tides are the periodic rise and fall of the ocean surface (occurring 
roughly twice daily), caused by the gravitational attraction of the moon and 
sun. 
7. Oxygen Concentration: Highest at the surface level where atmospheric 
exchange occurs; oxygen concentration decreases with depth, becoming 
practically non-existent in extreme depths. 
8. Hydrogen Ion Concentration (pH): Seawater is alkaline in nature with a 
typical pH ranging between 8.0 and 9.0 near the surface. 
9. Waves: Movements of surface waters caused by winds that facilitate 
continuous mixing of seawater near the surface. 
10. Light Penetration: Light penetrates ocean water up to a maximum depth 
of about 200 meters (Euphotic zone), directly limiting plant life to these upper 
illuminated layers.

Distribution of organisms in habitats  
Organisms in marine environments are distributed into three main ecological 
groups: 
1. Plankton (Floating organisms): Drifting microscopic organisms found 
mainly in the lighted upper zone. 
Phytoplankton (Producers): Microscopic plant-like algae (e.g., Diatoms, 
Dinoflagellates) that carry out photosynthesis. 
Zooplankton (Consumers): Microscopic animals (e.g., Protozoa, 
Copepods, jellyfish larvae). 
2. Nekton (Active swimmers): Animals capable of swimming strongly 
against water currents (e.g., Tilapia, Tuna, Sharks, Whales, Sea Turtles, 
Squids). 
3. Benthos (Bottom dwellers): Organisms attached to or crawling on the 
sea floor (e.g., Sea Anemones, Corals, Sea Urchins, Crabs, Starfish, Sea 
Cucumbers). 
Adaptive features of marine organisms  
Plant Adaptations 
Salt Water Swamp Plants: Possess succulent stems/roots to dilute salt 
concentration. 
Have pneumatophores (breathing roots) for atmospheric gas exchange. 
Stilt roots provide structural anchorage in mud. 
Viviparous seedlings ensure survival and continuity. 
Excrete excess salt to maintain internal body salt balance. 
Seaweeds: Holdfast structures for firm attachment to rocks. 
Mucilaginous outer layer prevents desiccation. 
Divided leaves and air bladders for buoyancy in water. 
Animal Adaptations 
Barnacles: Possess protective mantles or attachment pads to firmly anchor 
onto rocks, preventing desiccation during low tide. 
Cartilaginous Fishes (e.g., Sharks, Dogfish): Maintain high internal salt 
balance and body osmotic pressure to adapt to high ocean salinity. 
Bony Fishes (e.g., Tilapia, Herring): Drink salt water to cope with high 
salinity and utilize salt-secreting glands in their gills or eyes to excrete excess 
salt. 
Shrimps: Equipped with powerful chelipeds (claws) for capturing and 
holding prey. 
Crabs: Burrow rapidly into coastal mud to protect against wave action, 
tides, and predators. 
Periwinkles: Possess modified lungs for breathing air during exposure and 
a strong muscular foot for firm attachment. 
Starfish: Utilize tube feet to hold securely onto rocky shores and hard 
outer shells to prevent desiccation.

Types of Estuarine Habitat 
An estuarine habitat (or estuary) is a semi-enclosed coastal body of water 
where fresh water from rivers and streams meets and mixes with salt water 
from the ocean. This mixing produces brackish water, which has a salinity 
intermediate between fresh water (<0.5‰) and full seawater (~35‰). 
Estuaries are highly dynamic transition zones (ecotones) affected by tidal 
fluctuations, high nutrient loads, and fluctuating environmental parameters. 
Types of Estuaries 
1. Delta: A delta is where a river divides into many channels before entry 
into the ocean or sea. Brackish water or estuary (delta) is formed at the 
mouth of a river as it enters the sea. 
2. Lagoon: This is a body of ocean water that enters into the land through a 
canal and therefore has the opportunity of mixing with fresh water from 
rivers and streams. 
3. Bay: This is a little or small portion of the sea water which enters into the 
land and mixes up with fresh water from rivers and streams. A bay is very 
small and not long enough to rejoin the sea at another end. 
Characteristics of Estuarine Habitats 
The following are the principal characteristics of estuarine habitats: 
(1) Fluctuation in salinity: Salinity fluctuates in this habitat. Salinity is 
lower at the mouth of a river and gets higher towards the sea. Salinity is 
also affected by season: while the rainy season reduces salinity due to the 
addition of fresh water, the dry season increases it. 
(2) Turbidity: Turbidity of estuarine habitats increases especially during 
the rainy season when lots of debris are brought down by rivers to the 
habitat. This high turbidity also reduces the rate of photosynthesis and 
respiration by organisms. 
(3) Shallowness of water: Unlike sea water which is deep, the water in an 
estuarine habitat is generally very shallow. 
(4) Low species diversity: The estuarine habitat has low diversity of 
species compared to marine habitats. Common plant species include 
phytoplankton, algae, and marsh vegetation, while animal species include 
crabs, oysters, lobsters, etc. 
(5) Water is affected by tides: Sea water usually flows rapidly into 
estuaries at high tides and rushes back into the ocean at low tides. 
(6) High level of nutrients: The estuarine habitats contain abundant 
nutrients, especially organic detritus, which form the bulk of producers in

(7) Low oxygen content: Oxygen content of estuarine habitats is generally 
very low and, as a result, much of the microbiological activity is anaerobic. 
Distribution of Plants and Animals in Estuarine Habitats 
Plant Species and Their Adaptive Features 
(1) Planktons (diatoms): They possess air spaces in their tissues, rhizoids 
or false feet for attachment to rocky shores, and air bladders for buoyancy. 
(2) Algae: They possess chlorophyll for photosynthetic activities and a small 
size or large surface-area-to-volume ratio for floating. 
(3) Red mangrove (Rhizophora racemosa): It has stilt roots which grow 
down from the stem into the soft mud and develop numerous rootlets with 
air spaces for conducting air to root tissues. The roots also provide support 
and prevent plants from being washed away by tides. Furthermore, the 
seeds of red mangrove germinate while still on the parent plant (vivipary), 
preventing seedlings from being carried away by water currents. 
(4) White mangrove (Avicennia nitida): It has pneumatophores (breathing 
roots) for gaseous exchange. 
Animal Species and Their Adaptive Features 
(1) Mosquito larvae and pupae: These organisms possess breathing 
trumpets for gaseous exchange. 
(2) Crabs: They can burrow fast into the mud for protection against 
predators, strong waves, or tides. 
(3) Water snails and shrimps: These animals can burrow into the mud 
when the tide is going out, thus escaping the periodic dilution of their 
external medium. 
(4) Worms: They have strong, protective, impermeable coverings against 
high salinity. 
(5) Mudskippers: These animals have modified fins adapted for crawling 
when on land and for swimming when in water. 
(6) Fishes: Fishes like Tilapia have fins for movement and a swim bladder 
for buoyancy control. 
Food Chain in Estuarine Habitats 
A typical food chain in an estuarine habitat may contain up to three, four, or 
five trophic levels. Phytoplankton (such as diatoms) and organic detritus form 
the primary producers supporting the food chain. 
Examples of Food Chains in Estuarine Habitats: 
(i)   Detritus → Worms → Snails → Birds 
(ii)  Diatoms → Shrimps → Fishes 
(iii) Phytoplankton → Barnacles → Fish → Birds

Types and characteristics of fresh water habitat 
Fresh water habitat is a body of water formed mainly from inland waters and 
contain a very low level of salinity. Examples of fresh water habitat are rivers, 
ponds, streams, springs and lakes. Freshwater habitats are classified primarily 
based on the mobility or movement direction of the water body into two distinct 
categories: Lotic (Running) Waters and Lentic (Stagnant) Waters. 
Lotic (Running) Waters: Waters that flow continuously in a specific direction. 
They possess strong water currents, high oxygen concentration due to constant 
aeration, and are generally non-stratified. Examples are Rivers, streams, 
springs, brooks, waterfalls. 
Lentic (Stagnant) Waters: Standing or calm waters that do not flow in a 
specific direction. They accumulate organic sediments, undergo thermal/light 
stratification, and exhibit distinct zonation. Examples are Lakes, ponds, 
swamps, dams, reservoirs, bogs. 
Characteristics of Fresh Water Habitats 
Low Salinity: Freshwater habitats contain very low levels of dissolved salts 
(typically around 0.5% or less), compared to oceans which average about 3.5% 
(35 parts per thousand). 
Small Relative Size: Freshwater habitats occupy a very small proportion of 
Earth's total water surface compared to marine habitats, which cover over 75% 
of the Earth's surface. 
Temperature Variations: Temperature fluctuates with seasonal changes, 
weather, and depth. In lentic bodies, surface water is slightly warmer than 
bottom mud during warm periods. 
High Dissolved Oxygen Content: Oxygen is readily available in most 
freshwater zones, particularly in fast-flowing lotic waters and the surface layer 
of lentic bodies. 
Shallowness: Most freshwater habitats are relatively shallow compared to 
marine oceans, allowing light to easily penetrate to the substrate in many 
areas. 
Seasonal Volume Variation: Streams and rivers experience high volume, swift 
currents, and increased turbidity during rainy seasons, but dry up or shrink 
significantly in dry seasons. 
Current Action: Unidirectional currents in lotic environments distribute 
dissolved gases, salts, nutrients, and microorganisms while acting as a 
physical challenge to non-adapted organisms. 
Ecological Zones of Fresh Water Habitats 
1. Lentic Habitat Zones (e.g., Lakes and Ponds) 
Lentic habitats display two primary vertical ecological zones based on light 
penetration, rooted vegetation, and depth: 
Littoral Zone: The shallow edge/margin of the water body where sunlight 
easily penetrates to the bottom sediment. It features rooted vegetation, warm 
temperatures, maximum dissolved oxygen, and the highest primary biological 
productivity. 
Plants: Spirogyra, Chlamydomonas, water fern, duckweed, water lettuce 
(Pistia), diatoms, sedges. 
Animals: Water fleas, water snails, flatworms, frogs, toads, water skaters 
(Gerri’s), ducks, snakes, crocodiles, tadpoles, Hydra, hippopotamus. 
Benthic Zone: The deep bottom region composed of organic mud and 
substrate (humus). Although rooted vegetation like littoral plants is absent at 
the base, floating plants with deep-anchored roots exist here. Light is limited, 
and organic decomposition is high. 
Plants: Water lily (Nymphaea), water arum, ferns, Crinum lily, Commelina, 
submerged water grasses. 
Animals: Protozoa, rotifers, Hydra, tilapia fish, mudfish, catfish, leeches, 
caddisfly larvae, mosquito larvae/pupae, water spider, crayfish, water scorpion, 
water boatman, water bugs. 
2. Lotic Habitat Zones (e.g., Rivers and Streams) 
In lotic (running) water ecosystems, zones are differentiated by water flow 
velocity and pool depth: 
Pool Zone: Deeper regions of the river where water flow is relatively slow, calm, 
and peaceful. Fine silt and organic matter settle here. 
Rapid Zone: Shallow areas where water flows swiftly over rocky substrates. 
Aeration is maximum, but thermal stratification does not occur due to 
continuous mixing. 
Eutrophication in Freshwater Ecosystems 
Eutrophication is defined as the excessive growth of plant life or algal blooms 
in a body of water, caused by an enrichment of organic nutrients and minerals, 
ultimately leading to severe oxygen depletion and the collapse of aquatic life. 
Major Causes of Eutrophication: 
Overuse of agricultural fertilizers containing nitrates and phosphates, which 
wash into rivers/lakes during heavy rainfall. 
Discharge of untreated domestic sewage and organic waste runoff from fish 
farming operations.

Organisms inhabiting fresh waters face challenges such as osmotic water gain 
(due to hypotonic water), strong water currents in lotic habitats, and light 
limitations in deep lentic habitats. Below are their key structural, physiological,

Food Chains and Energy Flow in Fresh Waters 
Freshwater Food Chains 
Diatoms (Producer) → Fish fry (Primary Consumer) → Tilapia (Secondary 
Consumer) 
Detritus (Organic waste) → Worm (Primary Consumer) → Shrimps (Secondary 
Consumer) → Water Bird (Tertiary Consumer) 
Spirogyra (Producer) → Tadpoles (Primary Consumer) → Carps (Secondary 
Consumer) → Kingfisher (Tertiary Consumer) 
Energy Flow Pathway 
Radiant Solar Energy is absorbed by green plants and phytoplankton 
(producers) for photosynthesis to synthesize organic carbohydrates. 
Herbivores (zooplankton, tadpoles) consume producers directly (primary 
consumer level). 
Carnivores (small/large fish, water birds) obtain energy indirectly by feeding 
on herbivores. 
Decomposers (saprophytic fungi and bacteria) break down organic wastes and 
dead bodies, releasing inorganic nutrients back into the mud. 
Energy Loss: At each trophic transfer level, a significant proportion of energy is 
lost as heat through respiration and metabolic activities. 
Factors Affecting Fresh Water Habitats 
Freshwater ecosystems are regulated by both biotic and abiotic ecological 
factors: 
Biotic Factors: Producers (green plants, diatoms, algae), Consumers (fish, 
tadpoles, insects, aquatic birds), Parasites, and Decomposers (bacteria, 
saprophytic fungi). 
Abiotic Factors: Water current velocity, temperature, dissolved oxygen 
content, salinity level, water turbidity/clarity, pH, and sunlight penetration 
depth.

Features of Marsh 
Terrestrial habitats refer to life on earth. The nature of soil, rainfall and 
temperature are the major factors affecting the nature of terrestrial habitats. 
Terrestrial habitat is divided into four groups namely: Marsh, Rainforest, 
Savanna or Grassland and Arid.  
Marshy Habitats 
Marshy habitat is defined as a lowland habitat which is usually flooded or 
water logged all the time. Naturally, grasses and shrubs grow in marsh but 
when trees grow there, it is called a swamp. Marsh is often regarded as a 
transition between the aquatic and terrestrial habitats. 
Features of Marsh Habitats: 
1. Waterlogged & Anaerobic Soil: The ground remains saturated with 
water for extended periods, creating an oxygen-depleted (anaerobic) 
underground environment that slows organic decomposition. 
2. Herbaceous Plant Dominance: Trees are largely absent; non-woody 
plants with soft, flexible stems dominate the landscape. 
3. Shallow Water Level: Water depth typically ranges from a few 
centimeters to about a meter, allowing sunlight to reach the substrate 
and support dense vegetation. 
4. High Organic Accumulation: Partially decayed plant material 
accumulates to form nutrient-rich mud, silt, and peat deposits. 
5. Dynamic Salinity Levels: Depending on geographical location, salinity 
can range from completely fresh to brackish or hypersaline coastal water 
Formation of Marshes (Ecological Succession) 
Stage 1: Sediment Accumulation: Slow moving rivers, streams, or coastal tides 
deposit fine silt, clay, and organic debris in low lying shallow water basins. 
Stage 2: Shallowing of water: Continuous deposition reduces water depth, 
enabling sunlight to penetrate the basin floor and creating ideal conditions for 
plant attachment. 
Stage 3: Pioneer Colonization: water tolerant herbaceous species (e.g. reeds, 
sedges, cattails) take root in the soft mud, Marshes develop gradually through 
a process of ecological succession in areas with poor drainage and continuous 
sediment input: stabilizing the sediment with their root networks. 
Stage 4: Organic Maturation: As plant material dies and decays, organic peat 
accumulates, raising the ground level and establishing a mature marsh 
ecosystem. 
Types of Marshes 
There are two types of marshes. These are freshwater marshes and salt water 
marshes. 
Freshwater marshes: Fresh water marshes occur on land, just beyond the 
limits of the salt water marshes and beyond the areas influenced by tide. In 
this area, fresh water from rivers overflows the river banks to flood the 
adjoining lowlands resulting in the formation of fresh water marshes. 
Saltwater marshes: Saltwater marshes occur along the coastal areas and they 
are influenced by tides because the water along the coast is salty, it mixes up 
with fresh water from rivers to form brackish water. The action of tide in the 
ocean causes the flooding of adjoining lowlands with brackish water resulting 
in the formation of salt water marshes.
