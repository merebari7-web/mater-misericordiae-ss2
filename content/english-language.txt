Definition of Nominalization
Nominalization is the grammatical process of forming a noun from other word classes,
primarily verbs and adjectives. It is the process of turning a verb, or adjective, or sometimes
another word into a noun.
• Nominalization of Verbs: Verbs are converted into nouns using specific suffixes such
as -tion, -ment, -ance, -al, -ence, or -ing.
Examples: Decide → Decision | Govern → Government | Perform → Performance |
Refuse → Refusal | Attend → Attendance
• Nominalization of Adjectives: Adjectives are converted into nouns using suffixes such
as -ness, -ty, -ance, -ence, or -dom.
Examples: Happy → Happiness | Honest → Honesty | Free → Freedom | Patient →
Patience | Wide → Width

Consonant sounds
Consonant sounds are speech sounds produced with an obstruction or closure of airflow
in the vocal tract. In phonetics, a crucial distinction between consonant sounds is
voicing. This refers to whether the vocal cords vibrate when the sound is produced.
• Voiceless Sounds: The vocal cords are apart and do not vibrate. The sound is
produced by air only (like a whisper). Place your fingers on your throat; you will
feel no vibration.
• Voiced Sounds: The vocal cords are close together and vibrate. You will feel a
distinct buzzing sensation in your throat.
Practical Test: Say "ssssss" (like a snake). You feel nothing. Now say "zzzzzz" (like
a bee). You feel a vibration. /s/ is voiceless; /z/ is voiced.
Many English consonants form pairs where the mouth position is identical; the only
difference is voicing.

Summary writing
Summary writing tests the ability to extract essential information from a longer text and
present it concisely without losing core meaning. It is the act of condensing longer piece of
text (or event, argument, etc.) into a shorter version that captures only the essential points, in
your own words.
Features of a good summary writing
• Identify Main Ideas: Read actively to find key points in each paragraph. Strip away
illustrations, examples, statistics, direct quotations, and filler language.
• Paraphrase (Use Your Own Words): Express extracted ideas in original sentence
structures and synonyms. Avoid lifting wholesale sentences from the passage.
•  Maintain Objectivity: Stick strictly to the author's facts. Do not insert personal
opinions, extra knowledge, or unstated assumptions.
• Write Complete & Direct Sentences: Ensure every summary point is a grammatically
sound, complete sentence, unless a specific bulleted fragment format is requested.

Vocabulary Associated with Human Body Systems

Building a strong vocabulary in specific registers, such as biology and health, is essential for
tackling comprehension passages on these topics. Understanding these terms will allow you
to read scientific texts with greater examples in sentence.
Word list
• Circulatory System: The system that circulates blood and lymph around the
body.

• Heart: The muscular organ that pumps blood through the circulatory system.
• Artery: A blood vessel that carries oxygenated blood away from the heart.
• Vein: A blood vessel that carries deoxygenated blood towards the heart.
• Capillary: A tiny, thin-walled blood vessel that enables the exchange of
water, oxygen, and nutrients between blood and tissues.
• Plasma: The pale-yellow liquid component of blood that holds blood
cells in suspension.
• Respiratory System: The system responsible for taking in oxygen and
expelling carbon dioxide.
• Lungs: The two primary organs of respiration, where gas exchange occurs.
• Diaphragm: The dome-shaped muscle at the base of the lungs that
contracts and flattens to draw air in.
• Trachea: The windpipe; a tube that connects the larynx to the
bronchi of the lungs.
• Nervous System: The body's command centre, processing and transmitting
information.

• Neuron: A specialised cell transmitting nerve impulses; a nerve cell.
• Synapse: The eurotransmitter.
• Spinal Cord: junction between two nerve cells, where impulses
pass by diffusion of a nThe cylindrical bundle of nerve fibres
enclosed in the spine, connecting nearly all parts of the body to the
brain.
• Skeletal System: The body's framework, composed of bones and connective
tissues.

• Skeleton: The internal framework of the body.
• Cartilage: A resilient and smooth elastic tissue, a rubber-like padding
that covers and protects the ends of long bones.
• Ligament: A short band of tough, flexible, fibrous connective tissue that
connects two bones or cartilages or holds together a joint.
• Tendon: A flexible but inelastic cord of strong fibrous collagen tissue
attaching a muscle to a bone.
Digestive System: The system by which ingested food is broken down and absorbed
• Oesophagus: The part of the alimentary canal that connects the
throat to the stomach.
• Stomach: The internal organ in which the major part of the
digestion of food occurs.
• Intestine: (Small and Large) The long, continuous tube running from the
stomach to the anus, where nutrients are absorbed.
• Liver: A large lobed glandular organ that secretes bile and is
active in the formation of certain blood proteins.

Plural nouns
There are regular and irregular nouns. Plural formation of regular nouns are formed by adding –s or –es (e.g., book → books, box → boxes). Irregular nouns plural are formed by the change
of the internal vowel (e.g.,  foot → feet, mouse → mice, datum → data, crisis → crises).
Compound nouns (father-in-law → fathers-in-law).

Noun phrase
A noun phrase is a group of words built around a noun (or pronoun) that function together as
a single unit in a sentence, typically as a subject, object, or complement. It includes the
noun itself plus any word that modify or describe it such as
▪ Articles: the, a, an
▪ Adjectives: big, red
▪ Determiners: this, my
▪ Prepositional phrases: of the house
▪ Other modifiers e.g., possessives: John’s car, her book. Quantifiers: some apples,
many people, three cats. Etc.
Examples:
• ‘’The old bridge’’ collapsed. (subject)
• I saw ‘’a very tall building.’’ (object)
• She is ‘’my best friend.’’ (complement)
Even a single word like ‘’dogs’ or ‘’Maria’’ counts as a noun phrase, since a noun phrase can
be as minimal as just the head noun with no modifiers.

WEEK THREE
TOPIC: Reading and Comprehension – Reading for Main Ideas and supporting Details

Reading for Main Ideas
Main idea is the core thought or central argument of a paragraph, usually found in the topic
sentence.
Supporting details
Supporting details are facts, examples, statistics, and descriptions that expand upon the main
idea.
Reading strategy: skim quickly to identify general topics; scan purposefully to separate
foundational points from decorative details.

What is an expository essay?
An expository essay is a formal piece of writing that aims to explain, describe, or inform
the reader about a topic. Its primary purpose is to present a balanced, objective analysis
based on facts. Unlike an argumentative essay, it does not seek to persuade the reader to
adopt a particular opinion, but rather to "expose" information clearly and logically.

Structure of an expository essay
A well-structured essay is built like a house: a strong foundation (introduction),
supporting walls (body paragraphs), and a solid roof (conclusion).
1. The Introduction (1 paragraph):

• Hook: Begin with a compelling statement, a rhetorical question, or a striking fact to
engage the reader.
• Background: Provide brief context or define key terms related to the topic.
• Thesis Statement: This is the cornerstone of your essay. It is a single, clear
sentence that states the main point or central argument you will explain.
2. The Body (Typically 3 paragraphs):

• Each paragraph should develop one distinct main point that supports your thesis.
• Topic Sentence: The first sentence of each body paragraph, which states the main idea
of that paragraph.
• Supporting Details: Use facts, statistics, examples, and logical explanations to
elaborate on your topic sentence.
• Concluding Sentence (Optional but recommended): Summarises the paragraph
and may provide a transition to the next point.
• Use Transitional Words: Words like Furthermore, Moreover, In addition,
Consequently, For instance ensure a smooth flow between ideas and paragraphs.
3. The Conclusion (1 paragraph):
• Restate Thesis: Rephrase your thesis statement in new words, reflecting the
explanation you have provided.
• Summarise Main Points: Briefly recap the key arguments from your body
paragraphs.
• Final Thought: Offer a broader implication, a final insight, or a recommendation based
on the information presented. Avoid introducing new ideas.

Features
• Objective Tone
• Logical Sequence
• Formal Language.

Definition of consonant cluster
A consonant cluster (also known as a consonant blend) is a sequence of two or more
consonant sounds that appear together in a syllable without any vowel sounds between
them. Mastering these clusters is crucial for clear, fluent, and natural-sounding English
pronunciation. A common                               error for learners is epenthesis—the unintentional
insertion of a vowel sound (typically the schwa /ə/) within a cluster, which breaks the
word unnaturally.
Example:
•  Correct: film (/fɪlm/)
• Incorrect (with epenthesis): fi-lim (/'fɪl.ɪm/)

Types of consonant cluster

i. Two-Consonant Clusters These are the most common and form the basis for
more complex clusters.
• Initial Clusters (Start of a word):
o /s/ + consonant: stay, sky, slow, swim, snow
o /l/ blends: blow, clean, fly, glass, play
o /r/ blends: bring, cry, dry, free, grow, pray, tree

• Final Clusters (End of a word):
o -st: last, past, test
o -nd: hand, send, find
o -sk: task, desk, ask
o -mp: jump, lamp, camp
o -ft: left, gift, soft
o -ld: gold, hold, child
ii. Three-Consonant Clusters These require careful articulation to avoid
skipping or adding sounds.
• Initial Clusters: These almost always begin with /s/.

o /spr/: spring, spray, sprout
o /str/: street, strong, strike
o /skr/: script, scream, scrub
o /spl/: splash, split, spleen
• Final Clusters: Often formed by adding inflectional endings like -s or -ed.
o -nts: tents, wants, pants
o -mps: jumps, lamps, pumps
o -sks: tasks, desks, masks
o -pts: scripts, adapts, excerpts
o -lps: helps, gulps, pulps

iii. Four (and Five) Consonant Clusters These are complex and typically occur
at the end of words. The key is to articulate each consonant cleanly and quickly
without inserting a vowel.
• -fths: twelfths (/twɛlfθs/), fifths (/fɪfθs/)
• -ngths: strengths (/strɛŋθs/), lengths (/lɛŋθs/)
• -ksts: texts (/tɛksts/), next (/nɛkst/)
• -mpst: glimpsed (/ɡlɪmpst/)
• -mpts: prompts (/prɒmpts/), attempts (/ə'tɛmpts.

What is reading for inference?
Inferencial reading is the ability to read 'between the lines' — drawing logical conclusions
based on indirect clues, context, and implied evidence rather than direct statements.
Key Techniques: Identify contextual clues, analyze diction and author tone, and ask what
is implied rather than stated explicitly.

Definition of pronoun
A pronoun is a word that takes the place of a noun or a noun phrase to avoid repetition
and make  sentences less cumbersome. Using pronouns effectively is fundamental to
fluent and coherent writing and speaking.
Example:
• Without Pronoun: The student said the student would bring the student's book.
• With Pronoun: The student said she would bring her book.

The Unstressed Vowel (Schwa /ə/)
The schwa sound /ə/ is the most common, neutral, and unstressed vowel sound in spoken
English. It occurs exclusively in weak, unstressed syllables.
Examples:
about (/əˈbaʊt/), teacher (/ˈtiːtʃə/), doctor (/ˈdɒktə/), agree (/əˈɡriː/), banana (/bəˈnɑːnə/).
